KoFLEURS-R: Improving Text and Audio Consistency in the Korean FLEURS-R Corpus

Abstract

We present KoFLEURS-R, a refined version of the Korean portion of FLEURS-R that improves text-audio consistency at the recording level. FLEURS-R improves the acoustic quality of FLEURS recordings but retains the recording scripts, which do not always match what was actually spoken. In particular, parenthetical expressions may be spoken or omitted depending on the speaker, resulting in transcription mismatches. To address this issue, we propose a script-constrained refinement method that generates transcription candidates from the original text and selects the most appropriate candidate using acoustic evidence from two Whisper models, a Korean wav2vec 2.0 model, and a connectionist temporal classification (CTC) forced aligner. Evaluation on 189 human-verified recordings shows that KoFLEURS-R reduces the phonetic-key-normalized micro character error rate (CER) from 1.54% to 0.21%, an 86.4% relative reduction. The method outperforms both the removal of all parenthetical content and unconstrained automatic speech recognition (ASR), and correctly classifies 33 of 34 manually verified parenthetical occurrences (97.1%). The results suggest that script-constrained refinement can reduce transcript mismatches without replacing the script with error-prone ASR output. The refined metadata and the refinement pipeline will be released.

Text-Audio Mismatches in FLEURS-R

Text-audio mismatches can be classified into three types (Fig. 1): omission, insertion, and substitution. Omission occurs when script content is not spoken; it frequently involves parenthetical content, which FLEURS normalization retains after removing the parentheses. Insertion occurs when speech contains content absent from the script, such as words added by the speaker. Substitution occurs when script content is spoken differently, typically as a result of a reading error.

Method Overview

Rather than replacing the original text with ASR output, our method selects from a closed set of candidates derived from the original sentence, so recognition errors cannot enter the text. For each recording, we choose slot options using phonetic edit distances to hypotheses from two Whisper models [9] and a Korean wav2vec 2.0 model [10], [11].

Examples

Each example compares two speakers of the same script. Spectrograms are log-mel with one color scale for all recordings; click one to enlarge it. Word boundaries come from forced alignment and may be imprecise.

1. Omission

a parenthetical spoken by one speaker, skipped by the other

For example, parenthetical content in a recording script may be read aloud by one speaker but omitted by another. Retaining the content would introduce unspoken words into the text of the latter recording, whereas removing it would omit spoken words from the former.

2. Insertion

a gloss read again in Hangul by one speaker

For example, “크레바스(crevasse)” may be read as “크레바스” (keurebaseu), with the gloss omitted, or as “크레바스 크레바스” (keurebaseu keurebaseu), with the gloss read in Hangul.

3. Substitution

a number read in two ways

Similarly, we define slots for numbers and acronyms, which may have multiple readings; for instance, “3” may be read as “삼” (sam) or “셋” (set).

Data and models

  • Audio and transcriptions: FLEURS-R [7], derived from FLEURS [6]; both CC BY 4.0. The English sentences under the examples are the FLEURS en_us sentences with the same id (CC BY 4.0).
  • Models: Whisper large-v3 and large-v3-turbo [9] and a Korean wav2vec 2.0 model give the recognition hypotheses; a CTC forced aligner [5], [12] gives the CTC likelihood and the word boundaries shown under the spectrograms. Checkpoints: kresnik/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-korean and MahmoudAshraf/mms-300m-1130-forced-aligner on Hugging Face. The forced-aligner model is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0.
  • Spectrograms: log-mel computed with librosa (n_fft 1024, hop 10 ms, 128 mel bands up to 8 kHz, fixed range −55 to 25 dB, magma color map).
